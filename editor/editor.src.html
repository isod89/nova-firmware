How to find the source

  1. Run the radio on its battery, unplugged from USB and chargers.
  2. Go to your breaker panel with the radio. Switch the main breaker off for a moment: if the noise stops, the source is in your home. Then switch circuits back on one at a time to find which one carries it.
  3. On medium wave, walk around and follow the level up. When it is high, change to 7, 14 or 28 MHz to pinpoint the device.
  4. Hold the radio a few centimetres from suspects: LED bulbs and dimmers, phone and laptop chargers, TVs, routers, solar inverters, aquarium pumps. Unplug one: if the level drops, you found it.

Read it right

  1. This is an estimate from radio static. It cannot give a distance or a direction, and it is not a safety device: follow the official weather alerts.
  2. Stronger crashes and more of them over 15 minutes mean the storm is getting closer. Weaker or fewer mean it is moving away.
  3. Use it on battery, away from chargers, computers and LED lights. If the detector says Noisy, a device nearby is clicking: move away or find another frequency.
  4. If you hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck: go inside.

Marks

Mark a moment, then write what you heard.

Good practice

  1. Record here with "Start and stop with the session", or on a separate recorder started before the sweep: open that file afterwards and set when it started.
  2. Ask a question, press Q, then leave a few seconds of silence. Responses show how long after the question they came.
  3. For a blind test, turn on Blind mode so the radio's operator cannot see frequency or signal, and let someone else listen with headphones.
  4. Mark the moment you hear something, before you interpret it. Write the words in the notes afterwards, word for word.
  5. After the session, check each mark against the full log: a clear word on a strong, steady frequency is usually a broadcast station.
  6. Note the settings you used. Speed, direction and band change what you hear, so keep them in the report.
